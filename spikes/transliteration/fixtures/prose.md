# Prose fixture

Hand-written for the transliteration spike, in the register of long-form Islamic studies. Punctuation is written as it is typed ("straight" quotes, --, ...), and IJMES letters as the writer intends them to appear.

## Inheritance of knowledge

The Ḥanbalī jurist Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728/1328) argued that the ʿulamāʾ inherit the knowledge of the prophets, not their rank. His student Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya (d. 751/1350) carried the point further in *Iʿlām al-muwaqqiʿīn*, asking what a muftī owes to the one who asks him -- and what he owes to God. The question is old: al-Shāfiʿī (d. 204/820) had already asked it in the *Risāla*, and Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal answered it with his silence.

The phrase al-ʿulamāʾ warathat al-anbiyāʾ is usually cited as a ḥadīth, though its isnād is weak. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr records it in *Jāmiʿ bayān al-ʿilm wa-faḍlih*; al-Bukhārī gives it as a chapter heading without a full chain. Either way, the idea shaped how scholars in Baghdad, Baṣra and Iṣfahān understood their task: taʿlīm before taṣnīf, and iṣlāḥ before polemic.

## Dates and abbreviations

By 1000 A.H./1591 C.E. the madrasa curriculum had settled. Earlier, in the second century A.H., the boundaries between fiqh, ḥadīth and kalām were still being drawn. Western historians often date these debates to around 800 A.D., but the Hijrī calendar is the one the sources themselves use. A Ph.D. thesis from 1987 (now in several U.S. libraries) argues the curriculum was never fixed; the author cites an undated manuscript (n.d.) from Ṭāʾif. See also s.v. "ʿilm" in the second edition, vol. 3, pp. 1133--1134, and cf. the entry on taʾwīl. P.S. the catalogue number is wrong in the printed edition, i.e. it should read MS 4127.

Some Shīʿī authors add a blessing after the names of the Imams, e.g. Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq (a.s.), and Sunni authors write the Prophet (ṣ) or spell out ṣallā llāhu ʿalayhi wa-sallam. Both conventions appear in the same manuscript collections.

## Particles and compounds

IJMES joins prefixed particles with a hyphen: wa-l-ʿilm, bi-llāh, li-l-nās, fa-qāla, ka-dhālika, la-qad, sa-yaqūlu and wa-lā. The basmala, bi-smi llāhi l-raḥmāni l-raḥīm, shows elision as well. Compounds are hyphenated in English: the Sunni-Shiʿi divide, a Ḥanafī-Mālikī dispute, a twelfth-century debate, a well-known treatise, a so-called reform.

The Muʿtazila and the Ashʿarīs disagreed about the createdness of the Qurʾān. Al-Ashʿarī (d. 324/935) left the Muʿtazilī circle of al-Jubbāʾī, and the Māturīdīs of Samarqand reached similar conclusions by another route. In the Ẓāhirī school of Ibn Ḥazm, the text is read for its plain sense -- no qiyās, no taqlīd -- which made him a sharp critic of both.

## Names

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAwf, ʿĀʾisha bt. Abī Bakr, Fāṭima al-Zahrāʾ, ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭālib, ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb and ʿUthmān b. ʿAffān appear in every chapter. So do al-Ṭabarī, al-Ḍaḥḥāk b. Muzāḥim, Muqātil b. Sulaymān and Ibn ʿAbbās. In Sūrat Āl ʿImrān (Q 3:7) the muḥkam and mutashābih verses are distinguished; the commentators disagree about where to pause in the recitation...and the disagreement matters.

The word ẓulm means wrongdoing; ṭahāra is ritual purity; ḍalāl is going astray; ṣalāh is prayer; ḥilm is forbearance. A muʿallim teaches; a muḥaddith transmits; a faqīh reasons. The suʾāl of a student and the jawāb of a teacher form the basic unit of the genre.

## Quotation and voice

"The scholar who does not act on his knowledge," al-Ghazālī wrote, "is like a lamp that lights the way for others while burning itself." It's a line he didn't invent, but he made it his own. Later readers asked: 'Did he mean the jurists, or himself?' Ibn al-Jawzī's answer -- in *Talbīs Iblīs* -- was harsh.

He quotes the verse «إِنَّمَا يَخْشَى اللَّهَ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ الْعُلَمَاءُ» and then the Arabic proverb "العلم بلا عمل كالشجر بلا ثمر". The ʿālim and the ʿābid are not rivals.

## Transliteration in running prose

A footnote may mix systems: older works write Koran, Mahomet and Hadeeth, while recent ones write Qurʾān, Muḥammad and ḥadīth. Students learn to read both. The difference between ʿ and ʾ is not cosmetic: saʾala (to ask) and saʿala (to cough) are different verbs, and the ʿayn in Shīʿa is not the hamza in Shīʾa (which is not a word at all).

Typing these well matters... especially in a long book with thousands of names. A writer working on al-Mughnī or al-Muwaṭṭaʾ, on the ʿAqīda Ṭaḥāwiyya or on the Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn, should not have to stop for each mark.
